How Take-Home Pay Is Calculated

Your take-home pay is what's left of your gross salary after all taxes and pre-tax deductions are subtracted. For most W-2 employees in the US, four main deductions determine your net pay: federal income tax, state income tax, Social Security tax, and Medicare tax.

Federal Income Tax

The US uses a progressive federal income tax system with seven brackets ranging from 10% to 37%. You only pay the higher rate on income within that bracket, not on your entire salary. Your filing status (single, married filing jointly, head of household) determines which bracket thresholds apply to you. The standard deduction ($14,600 for single filers, $29,200 for married filing jointly in 2024) reduces your taxable income before brackets are applied.

State Income Tax

State income taxes vary widely. Nine states (Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, and Wyoming) have no state income tax at all. Others range from flat rates as low as 1.95% (North Dakota) to progressive systems topping out at 13.3% (California). Your state of residence determines which rates apply to your income.

FICA Taxes (Social Security & Medicare)

FICA stands for the Federal Insurance Contributions Act. Every W-2 employee pays 6.2% for Social Security on wages up to $168,600 (2024 cap) and 1.45% for Medicare on all wages. If you earn above $200,000 ($250,000 for married filing jointly), an additional 0.9% Medicare surtax applies to wages above that threshold. Your employer matches these amounts, but only the employee share is deducted from your paycheck.

Pre-Tax Deductions

Contributions to employer-sponsored retirement plans like 401(k) or 403(b) accounts reduce your federal and state taxable income, meaning you pay less in income taxes now. Health insurance premiums paid through your employer's cafeteria plan (Section 125) also reduce your taxable income. HSA and FSA contributions work similarly. These deductions can meaningfully increase your take-home pay.

Tips to Increase Your Take-Home Pay

  • Maximize retirement contributions: 401(k) contributions (up to $23,000 in 2024, or $30,500 if 50+) reduce your taxable income dollar-for-dollar.
  • Use an HSA if eligible: Contribute up to $4,150 (individual) or $8,300 (family) pre-tax in 2024.
  • Check your W-4: If you consistently get large tax refunds, you may be over-withholding. Adjusting your W-4 increases each paycheck.
  • Use dependent care FSA: Up to $5,000 per year in pre-tax childcare expenses.
  • Consider your state: If you work remotely, living in a no-income-tax state can save thousands annually.
