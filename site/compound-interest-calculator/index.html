How Compound Interest Works

Compound interest is interest calculated on both the initial principal and the accumulated interest from previous periods. Unlike simple interest, which only earns interest on the original amount, compound interest allows your earnings to generate their own earnings — creating exponential growth over time.

The formula for compound interest is:

A = P(1 + r/n)nt + PMT × [((1 + r/n)nt - 1) / (r/n)]

Where P is principal, r is the annual rate, n is the compounding frequency, t is time in years, and PMT is the periodic contribution. This calculator handles all of this for you automatically.

The Rule of 72

The Rule of 72 is a quick mental math shortcut to estimate how long it takes for an investment to double. Simply divide 72 by the annual interest rate:

  • At 6% interest: 72 / 6 = 12 years to double
  • At 8% interest: 72 / 8 = 9 years to double
  • At 10% interest: 72 / 10 = 7.2 years to double
  • At 12% interest: 72 / 12 = 6 years to double

This rule works best for interest rates between 6% and 10%. For rates outside that range, 69.3 divided by the rate gives a more accurate result.

How Compounding Frequency Matters

The more frequently interest is compounded, the more you earn. Here is how different frequencies compare on a $10,000 investment at 7% over 10 years (with no additional contributions):

  • Annually: $19,671.51
  • Quarterly: $19,897.89
  • Monthly: $20,096.61
  • Daily: $20,137.53

While daily compounding earns more than annual, the difference between daily and monthly compounding is relatively small. Most savings accounts and investments compound daily or monthly.

The Power of Starting Early

Time is the most powerful factor in compound interest. Starting 10 years earlier can dramatically change your outcomes, even with smaller contributions. For example, investing $300/month starting at age 25 at 7% annual return grows to approximately $734,000 by age 65. Waiting until age 35 to start the same investment yields only about $340,000 — less than half, despite contributing for only 10 fewer years.

Why Monthly Contributions Matter

Regular monthly contributions have a massive impact on long-term wealth building. Even small amounts add up significantly thanks to compound interest. Contributing an additional $100 per month at 7% over 30 years adds over $120,000 to your final balance — far more than the $36,000 you actually contributed.

Accounting for Inflation

While your nominal returns might look impressive, inflation erodes purchasing power over time. At a typical 3% inflation rate, a dollar today is worth about 74 cents in 10 years and 55 cents in 20 years. This calculator includes an inflation-adjusted figure so you can see what your future balance is actually worth in today's purchasing power. The "real return" (after inflation) is what truly matters for your financial planning.
